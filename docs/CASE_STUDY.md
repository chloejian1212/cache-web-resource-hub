## Overview

The CACHE resource hub project addressed a practical information problem: useful climate, health, and equity resources needed a clearer entry experience for visitors with different backgrounds and goals.

Our team worked across research, resource curation, information architecture, website improvements, and handoff planning. The delivered website presents topic-based navigation, introductory learning material, and expandable lists of further resources. Readers can explore the [published hub](https://carolinaadvocates.org/resources) and the [Education & Training page](https://carolinaadvocates.org/resources-et) directly.

## 1. The challenge: one collection, different reasons to visit

A practitioner might need material for a patient conversation. A student may want a clear introduction to an unfamiliar topic. A research-oriented visitor may need detailed sources, while another visitor wants to understand the organization and find a practical next step.

Those needs create a design tension. A small collection can offer direction but insufficient depth; a large list can preserve breadth but leave visitors unsure where to begin. Visual polish alone does not solve that decision problem.

Stakeholder walkthroughs also identified wider website concerns, including navigation, membership and newsletter pathways, older content, and consistency across browsers and devices. The resource-discovery experience became a useful focus for connecting research with practical page improvements.

**Design question:** How might we give visitors a useful starting point without closing off deeper exploration?

## 2. Research and discovery

The team developed interview questions and task-based usability materials, collected qualitative responses, and synthesized practitioner needs into personas. Stakeholder discussions and resource inventories provided organizational context.

The research explored expectations of CACHE, credibility, preferred depth, useful resource formats, organization, and successful visits. The usability materials covered understanding the site, getting involved, locating resources, and supporting the organization.

The resulting notes supported several themes:

| Theme | Design implication |
| --- | --- |
| People need different depths of information. | Create a clear introduction with a route to further reading. |
| Visitors want to judge relevance and trust before opening a link. | Use meaningful titles, source context, and helpful descriptions. |
| Format preferences depend on the situation. | Make formats recognizable and support practical use. |
| Audience grouping helps some visitors but may restrict others. | Retain a common browsing path rather than requiring an audience identity. |
| A small organization must maintain the result over time. | Fit the structure to its existing platform and document ongoing care. |

These are qualitative themes. The [research summary](RESEARCH_SUMMARY.md) preserves the differences between participants’ preferences and outlines what remains to be measured.

## 3. Turning research into an experience

### A topic-based way into the collection

The published resource hub presents five primary topics: clinical impacts, health-system decarbonization, education and training, equity and justice, and severe-weather preparedness. Each has an image, a short description, and a dedicated destination. The Resources menu also includes planetary health.

This gives visitors a shared starting point without requiring them to select a professional identity before seeing content. That direction is consistent with the research tension around audience categories, although the available evidence does not establish every step of the team’s decision history.

The published page names differ from the early six-category resource inventory. I document the actual navigation separately from that historical taxonomy in the [information architecture](INFORMATION_ARCHITECTURE.md).

### A clear first layer, followed by more depth

The Education & Training page begins with a topic introduction and selected basic learning material. It then offers further web resources through an expandable group.

That structure addresses the core design tension: visitors have something concrete to start with, while a larger collection remains available. Other reviewed topic pages use the same broad pattern and separate additional content by format where appropriate.

The website review supports describing this hierarchy as delivered. It does not establish that visitors now complete their tasks faster; that remains an evaluation question.

### Further resources organized by format

Several topic pages group additional material as academic articles, web resources, and videos. Education & Training uses a web-resource group, while planetary health provides article and web-resource groups.

The Education & Training group was opened during the review: its list became visible and its control changed to an expanded state. The [live-site document](LIVE_SITE.md) includes a screenshot of that interaction.

[![Expanded Education and Training resource list](../assets/screenshots/education-training-resources-expanded.jpg)](https://carolinaadvocates.org/resources-et)

### Navigation between levels and topics

Related-topic links give visitors a way to continue when their initial topic does not match their needs. Return controls provide a route toward the hub. The review also found two link defects, which are recorded as concrete maintenance items rather than hidden behind a claim that all navigation works.

## 4. Iterating with stakeholder feedback

April 2026 notes requested a clearer emphasis on introductory resources, relevant imagery and descriptions, removal of the resource carousel, and lists for further material.

The current Education & Training page visibly uses introductory content followed by an expandable list. This is a useful connection between the recorded feedback and the published design. Without a complete version history, it would be inappropriate to assign an exact implementation date or claim a verified before-and-after transformation for each element.

## 5. Working within Squarespace

CACHE wanted to retain its existing platform. The team’s work therefore had to serve both visitors and future editors: a useful resource collection should not depend on a maintenance workflow the organization cannot sustain.

The published experience relies on topic pages, content sections, ordinary links, embedded media, and expandable resource groups. More ambitious ideas from the research, such as audience, region, or format filtering, should be treated as future possibilities unless they are implemented and tested separately. No such custom filtering is claimed here.

This repository documents the work. The client’s hosted website and administration remain in Squarespace and are not reproduced as application source in this repository.

## 6. Planning for continued use

I contributed to the sustainability section of the proposal, covering ownership, editing documentation, recurring resource review, and continuity for future maintainers or student teams.

That planning becomes more concrete once a site is live. The review found that the Education & Training return control still used an old hub address, and a related-topic link on the decarbonization page pointed to the current page rather than the topic named in its label. These are examples of the ongoing work a resource hub needs even after publication.

The [maintenance guide](MAINTENANCE.md) develops the original plan into a recommended workflow, including checks for link destinations and consistency between hub entries, menus, and topic pages. It does not claim that every suggested governance step has been adopted by the organization.

## 7. Outcomes

The project has a publicly available resource hub with dedicated topic pages. Observable deliverables include:

- A topic-based entry experience with images and descriptions.
- Selected introductory material before further resource lists.
- Format-based resource groups on several topic pages.
- A working expanded resource list on Education & Training.
- Research, categorization, iteration feedback, and maintenance documentation that explain the work.

Publication demonstrates delivery, while user impact requires a separate evaluation. The available materials do not establish a final usability score, shorter task times, increased conversions, or higher traffic. The interface review also does not substitute for a full accessibility or cross-browser audit.

## Reflection

The main lesson for me was to design the decisions between pieces of content. Choosing a topic, recognizing an introductory resource, and deciding whether to go deeper are distinct moments that deserve clear support.

The live outcome makes that structure visible. It also shows why maintenance belongs in the design process: a page can be understandable while a misplaced link interrupts the journey. A future evaluation should combine realistic resource-finding tasks with consistent observations of success, hesitation, and navigation, while the content team continues to review the resource collection.
